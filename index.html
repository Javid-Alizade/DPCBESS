Differentiable Predictive Control of Battery Storage for Voltage Regulation in a Distribution Grid#

Javid Alizade (M.Sc. student, Istanbul Gedik University)

Important

Preliminary, validation part only. Test part sealed, not yet evaluated.

Results · Decisions and protocols · How to reproduce · Code repository: not published yet

Abstract#

Rooftop solar can change its output within minutes. In a distribution feeder, fast changes in solar power move bus voltages towards or beyond the ±5% limits. Battery inverters can help, through both active power (P) and reactive power (Q), but deciding their set-points every few minutes with an online optimiser can be slow on large feeders. This project studies Differentiable Predictive Control (DPC): a small neural-network policy is trained offline by backpropagating through a differentiable model of the grid (DistFlow power flow), the batteries and a safety layer. Once trained, the policy needs only one forward pass per control step. The project is an open, reproducible implementation on the IEEE 33-bus feeder with two batteries and four PV plants, using real, openly licensed solar and load measurements. It compares DPC with no battery action, a local Volt-VAR droop rule and an online model predictive controller (MPC). The results shown here are preliminary: they come from the validation part of the data, and the test part has not been opened.

Problem#

  • Grid. IEEE 33-bus radial feeder (Baran and Wu, 1989), per-unit system, bus 0 is the substation. Two load levels are always reported: a base scenario with the load scaled by 0.7 and a stress scenario at the standard load (×1.0).

  • Disturbances. 3.9 MW of PV at buses 14, 17, 29 and 32 and time-varying load at every bus, at 5-minute resolution.

  • Actuators. Two batteries at buses 17 and 32, 0.5 MW / 1 MWh each, which can set both P and Q (inverter rating 0.5 MVA). A positive P means discharge, so the net consumption at a bus is load − solar − P.

  • Goal. Keep all bus voltages inside 0.95-1.05 pu, and close to 1 pu, with low network losses and modest battery use, in particular around extreme solar ramps.

  • Research questions (from the proposal; RQ2 reworded, see the caveats):

    • RQ1. How can nonlinear DistFlow power flow, inverter constraints and battery dynamics be represented as differentiable computation graphs, and how do gradients propagate through the closed loop over a finite horizon?

    • RQ2. To what extent can physics-informed penalties combined with a differentiable feasibility projection improve voltage margin and reduce voltage violations during extreme solar-ramp events, compared with black-box deep reinforcement learning and online MPC?

    • RQ3. How effectively can DPC include battery stress and degradation costs in policy training while keeping voltage regulation? (Not started; see Next steps.)

Method#

  1. Differentiable plant. Branch-flow DistFlow solved by a fixed number of backward/forward sweeps, written in PyTorch so that gradients pass through it (8 sweeps in training, 15 in evaluation). It is checked against pandapower.

  2. Policy. A multilayer perceptron (2 hidden layers of 128, Tanh) maps the current state to P and Q of both batteries. Inputs: state of charge (SoC), voltages at the battery buses on the previous step, total load, PV at the four PV buses, the latest solar ramp rate, the previous action and the time of day. No forecast of future load or solar is given.

  3. Safety layer. The raw action is limited in three steps: P is limited so that SoC stays in 10-90% within the step, then P and Q are clipped to their limits, then (P, Q) is scaled radially onto the 0.5 MVA circle if needed. The layer is differentiable. It enforces battery and inverter limits only; it does not guarantee voltage limits, which are penalised in the loss and measured afterwards.

  4. Training loss. The terms of the proposal’s eq. (16): network losses, voltage deviation from 1 pu, control effort, a soft SoC penalty and a soft voltage-limit penalty, plus a terminal SoC term with a 30-70% dead zone. Degradation and C-rate terms are off. The policy is rolled out over a horizon of 144 steps (12 h) and trained by backpropagation through time.

  5. Selection and evaluation. Candidates were compared by pre-specified rules on the validation part only, in a continuous closed loop over all validation steps. Every rule was written down, with its date, before the run it judged (Decisions).

  6. References. No battery action; a Volt-VAR droop rule (original setting, and a setting tuned on the training part); an online MPC with a convex LinDistFlow model and a persistence forecast; and an oracle MPC with exact future data, as an upper bound.

Data#

  • Solar. Visser et al. (2022), rooftop PV measurements from Utrecht, the Netherlands, Zenodo, doi:10.5281/zenodo.10953360, CC BY 4.0.

  • Load. Trindade (2015), ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014, UCI Machine Learning Repository, doi:10.24432/C58C86, CC BY 4.0.

  • Split by time, no overlap. Training: 18 months, validation: 6 months, test: 12 months. Solar and load years are paired by calendar, so they share no real weather. Gaps in the solar data are never filled; windows with missing data are skipped.

  • Extreme ramp. A step whose absolute change of total PV is in the top 1% of the training part.

Results (validation part, preliminary)#

Read these results with the following caveats

  • Preliminary, validation part only. The test part is sealed and has not been evaluated. Every number comes from one network (IEEE 33-bus) and from the validation part, which was also used to select the DPC policy, so the DPC numbers are optimistically biased.

  • Data. Solar (Utrecht, 2014-2017) and load (Portugal, 2012-2014) are real measurements, but they come from different places and years and share no real weather. The PV size of 3.9 MW was chosen after a no-control check on the training part only (at 2.6 MW no training step exceeded 1.05 pu).

  • RQ2 was reworded on 2026-11-04, before any controller existed, because extreme ramps and voltage violations rarely coincide in this data (see Decisions).

  • Training bias. 30% of the training windows contain an extreme solar ramp.

  • Differences from the proposal’s eq. (16). Degradation and C-rate terms are off in every variant (“degradation off”). A terminal SoC term with a 30-70% dead zone is added and is not in eq. (16).

  • The safety layer goes beyond the proposal: it also limits P by the stored energy (SoC kept within 10-90%), not only by the inverter limits of eqs. (4)-(7). It does not guarantee voltage feasibility.

  • Active power use is marginal and not seed-robust. Voltage is regulated mostly with reactive power Q; the selected policy passes the pre-specified “P is used” threshold on 1 of 3 seeds.

  • The MPC baseline is a convex LinDistFlow MPC with soft voltage limits and a lossless SoC model, not the AC-OPF MPC named in the proposal, and it was not tuned. Speed comparisons are therefore against a convex MPC. The oracle MPC uses exact future data and is an upper bound, not a real controller.

  • Ablations. “penalty-only” includes a soft inverter-limit penalty that was added on purpose (decision D1). Variants without the safety layer can break the inverter limits; such steps are physically impossible operation and are reported as shares, not as achievable performance.

  • Corrections to the proposal: a discharging battery enters the net load with a minus sign, and the left side of eq. (9) is P, not 2P. Results computed before the SoC energy limit was added (2026-11-05) are superseded and not shown.

The results are in seven notebooks. Each one only reads saved result files from the repository and draws tables and figures from them. Nothing is trained or optimised while the site is built.

Next steps#

  • Sealed test run. One run on the test part, with a protocol written down before it, reporting both load scenarios and all seeds. It has not been done.

  • RQ2 metrics (worst voltage margin, maximum deviation and time to the safe band around extreme ramps) are defined and implemented, but have not yet been computed for any controller.

  • Open: a deep reinforcement learning baseline and the degradation extension (RQ3). Neither has been started.

Reproducibility#

All code, configurations and result files are versioned. Every run records its configuration, random seed and git commit, and whether the code differed from that commit. The data are downloaded from their original sources with checksums. See How to reproduce.
